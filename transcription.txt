THE JOHN G. KERR HOSPITAL FOR THE INSANE
Canton, China

Report for the years 1910 and 1911

“All things shall become new.” Outwardly at least this has come to be largely true in China today. A new form of government, new officials, new customs, a new life and spirit, a new calendar—in fact this is a new China. Nor was there ever before such an opportunity to preach the Gospel for never before was there such a willingness to hear and read for one’s self.

[marginal-heading] Effects of the war

While on our quiet side of the river we have been thus far unmolested in our work, seeing nothing and hearing but little of the noise of battle waged elsewhere we have, nevertheless, felt keenly at several points the inconvenience arising out of the disturbed state of the country. A large part of the support for the patients is still outstanding; because in the general flight of the old time magistrates was one who was responsible for many patients who had been sent from his yamen. With the private cases it is also to some extent true. The price of foodstuffs has risen considerably. For this reason it has been necessary to raise the prices for board slightly. A number of soldiers have been received as patients, they having become insane since the war broke out. Following the times of greatest disturbance in Canton, many patients were taken away by their families amounting to a real exodus.

Much of the low-lying land has been filled in so that we are not in such dread of floods as formerly. For this purpose we used broken bricks and pottery, cinders and sand rather than earth, in order to ensure good drainage and rapid drying of the surface after rains, as patients like to sit and lie on the ground. [marginal-heading] Canals filled in
Concrete drains
Well digging

During the first year another epidemic of cholera broke out which was, happily, of short duration. This visitation brought home to us again the immediate need of a better water supply than the river and large canal just outside the grounds. The two dirty, unsightly canals within the grounds which menaced the health of all the patients and were directly dangerous to a certain number, were filled in completely and durable concrete drains built in their places. A well eighty-three feet deep was dug and drilled which sends its pure, sparkling water nearly up to the surface. From that level it is pumped up into a native-made, wooden tank having an elevation sufficient to deliver water to the ground floors although the piping has been no more than begun. We are glad to report that since these improvements were made we have had a very decided falling off in the number of cases of bowel trouble.

[marginal-heading] Walling in of new compound for women

Until this last year the men and women have occupied the same compound. But we were able this year to wall in a large part of the newly acquired property and thus provide one-half of the female patients with a compound of their own. This newly acquired property amounted to only one and a-half acres and was made up of six small pieces bought from as many separate owners, which illustrates one of the difficulties in buying land here.

[marginal-heading] Overcrowded
Many new buildings

Much of the time the hospital has been in an overcrowded condition and it has been necessary to build repeatedly in order to keep pace with the ever increasing number of patients. The last report stated that the Provincial Government and the Police Department had made gifts amounting to over $5000 and that other gifts from America and China had raised this amount to over $6000, Mex., available for building purposes. But the large building we hoped at that time to put up had to remain with only plans drawn and ground prepared; because when the bids were in, it appeared that the money on hand was too little for the task.

[marginal-heading] New patients coming continually
Old building made new

But meantime the patients were coming in and provision had to be made to receive them. An old brick dwelling house stood on the grounds consisting of a middle portion of two stories and two very “tumbled down” wings. It had already been used to house many patients. The two wings were torn down and built up new two stories high with the best of brick and reinforced concrete, making a good strong house of two stories and many small rooms for renting, being larger than before the change.

[marginal-heading] Bund

One side of the hospital grounds lies along a canal. A fine, strong bund, six feet high, was erected for one-third of the distance,—110 feet—using rough granite blocks below and moulded concrete blocks above, the latter made by our own workmen.

[marginal-heading] Bath rooms

We were able early in the first year to move into the commodious, new kitchen located in the centre of the grounds. Adjacent to this were erected six rooms to be used for bathing and for washing clothes as well as an upper story to be used as cooks’ quarters. However, the increasing number of patients made it necessary until rather recently to use the bath rooms as sleeping rooms for patients.

Of all the 12 buildings which have been erected during these two years, nine are dwellings for patients, two are attendants’ quarters and one is the bath-rooms and cooks’ quarters. Two are built of boards, but ten of them are built of the very best of brick with concrete floors and tiled roofs and are in every way most substantial. They should last for fifty or one hundred years, at least, and with little outlay for repairs as they are of very simple design.

Thus building has been going on almost continually during these two years.

[marginal-heading] American flag

Finally, that it might be known to any and all of the many tens of thousands of soldiers that the hospital is an American institution, we have raised a fine flagstaff from which will fly the ensign of the new government at the top and the stars and stripes below, this disposition being dictated by courtesy to the country in which we are but guests. Americans and American institutions are in great favor among the Chinese at the present time. The beautiful, large flag is the work of Mrs. Allen’s skilful fingers.

[marginal-heading] Industries

This year we have introduced two new industries among the patients—the making of the warm, padded coats supplied in winter to all the officials’ cases, and the making of the strong baskets used by the patients for carrying sand etc. for filling in the low land.

Dr. Hofmann attended the meeting of the Medical Missionary Association of China at Hankow in 1910. He has had charge of all the building operations almost to the exclusion of medical work which has been a cause of disappointment to himself as well as to the superintendent. Nearly every missionary has to turn builder and superintend his own house-building. But it has been extreme in this case. With only two foreigners on the staff it could not be otherwise. All such building work needs the direct and constant oversight of a foreigner to ensure good workmanship.

[marginal-heading] Chinese physician

An important acquisition to the staff has been that of Dr. Ho Tsz Yiu, a Chinese gentleman taught medicine by Dr. Kerr, who has fitted nicely into a place which seems to have been left for him. We believe the Master sent him to us as the work of administration, medical attendance and building had come to be too much of a burden for the two foreign physicians to bear alone. He is being fitted also thus to take charge of some similar institution at some time in the future and at some other city.

[marginal-heading] Alcohol a cause of insanity

We note with sorrow the increasing number of cases of insanity of which the exciting cause is alcohol and a considerable number of cases of the acute delusional form of alcoholic psychosis. We fear it comes from the increased use of foreign liquors or, at least, because of habits acquired by contact with foreigners.

[marginal-heading] General paralysis of the insane

Another fact which has come to light is the large number of cases of general paralysis. In former years few such cases found their way here or, it may be, they were not all recognized. We were then surprised at the small number knowing, as we did, the great prevalence of syphilis. But during the last few years and particularly during the last year the number has increased markedly.

The symptoms are the same as elsewhere and the patients die as early, many within a few months after being received: because they are seldom brought at the beginning of the disease.

There remains still much to do for the comfort and welfare of the patients and for the accommodation of larger numbers.

[marginal-heading] Needs

We need first a good water-heating apparatus for large quantities of water.

Then we need two small buildings which can be warmed during the cold weather, one for the men and one for the women, to make more comfortable such as are feeble or who for other reasons need to be kept warm artificially.

We need more houses for the women so that they may all be removed to their own compound. This will leave just that amount of room free for men.

We need a water tower and water-piping to carry the pure well-water into all the many buildings.

We need money to complete the bund along the canal which will reclaim a considerable strip of land and prevent the compound wall from falling.

We greatly need an administration building.
We need a new chapel.
And we need very urgently money to make extensive repairs on the two large buildings first erected.

[marginal-heading] Wanted a physician

Another urgent need is that of a new foreign physician to take the place of the superintendent while on a very needed furlough and to remain thereafter as a permanent, additional member of the staff. A second new foreign physician will be needed in 1913 to take the place of Dr. Hofmann who will then go on his furlough but who plans, upon his return to China, to give his time entirely to teaching in the new union medical school.

HOSPITAL EVANGELISTIC WORK, 1910–1911

MRS. J. G. KERR

During the past two years the Evangelistic work has gone on without a break. Morning prayers, one of its most important features, occur daily except on Sabbath, conducted by Dr. Selden assisted by our native Evangelist. In the absence of our Superintendent at any time Dr. Hofmann takes his place. On Dispensing days the Evangelist and our two Bible Readers tell the old old story to the men and women who come for healing. From time to time there have been many who have come not for medicine but “to hear the doctrine” which has given us great encouragement. Two of these women have received baptism; a third died without having had an opportunity of confessing Christ but her trust was in Him and all fear of death, seemed taken away. Each Tuesday we have a prayer-meeting held especially for the women attendants which has proved a great blessing to us all. Those who have come to the Institution for employment only, having never heard of the one living and true God, are now many of them his professed followers. Wednesday evening the helpers both men and women gather and spend a half hour in singing, which they enjoy and which is most beneficial to our music at our various services. The singing is followed by a prayer meeting usually led by the Evangelist. On Thursday evening the members of the Brotherhood conduct a service of Bible study of their own. A Primary Sabbath School composed of Boat Children meet in the chapel every Sabbath morning at 8 a.m. The equipment for this work is not all we could wish but a great point is gained in getting hold of these waifs whose religious instruction is measured by this one hour, one day in the week. The mother of one of these children joined the church at our last communion. Miss McEwan of the New Zealand Mission has given valuable assistance
